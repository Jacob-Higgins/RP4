\begin{abstract}

An analytical model of the impact of plume impingement from a reaction control thruster onto a debris surface has been created. This scenario is relevant to the study of possible ways to detumble space debris objects prior to their capture in active debris removal missions. Simons' plume model has been used to estimate the local density, velocity, and temperature in the plume. A distance of 10m between the thruster and debris object has been assumed, and at this distance the flow densities are of the order of $10^{-8}kg/m^3$ for a 5N hydrazine monopropellant thruster. The plume was found to behave as a free-molecular flow at this distance. Subsequent analysis of the plume impingement onto a glass surface layer of a solar panel on the debris object, found peak heat fluxes of approximately $150W/m^2$, causing a temperature increase up to $14K$ during an example detumbling sequence. Peak stresses on the surface were found to be on the order of $1MPa$. Comparison of these results to failure limits related to adhesive melting and fracture propagation found the risk of debris breakup to be low. However, there is a large degree of uncertainty in the analytical model presented, and it is suggested that future work should focus on comparing these results to those generated by a numerical method, in particular the direct simulation monte carlo method.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec-introduction}

The accumulation of space debris presents a direct challenge to the sustainability of orbital operations and the long-term viability of space exploration. Space debris comprises non-functional satellites, spent rocket stages, and fragmentation products from orbital collisions or explosions. Debris typically travels at velocities exceeding 7 km/s in low Earth orbit (LEO). At such speeds, even millimetre-sized particles pose a significant risk to spacecraft operations; larger debris is capable of causing catastrophic collisions that generate large amounts of fragmentation debris \cite{esaspacedebrisofficeESASANNUALSPACE2024}. As of 2023, the European Space Agency (ESA) estimates that approximately 40,500 objects larger than 10 cm, 1.1 million objects between 1 cm and 10 cm, and over 130 million objects smaller than 1 cm are currently orbiting Earth \cite{europeanspaceagencySpaceEnvironmentStatistics25}. The most congested regions are LEO (below 2,000 km) and geostationary orbit (GEO, approximately 35,786 km), where there is a particular need for enhanced tracking, collision avoidance, and debris mitigation strategies such as active debris removal (ADR). Active debris removal (ADR) is defined as the capability to interact with passive orbiting objects, in order to reduce their orbital lifetime  \cite{steeringgroupIADCStatementActive2022a}. It is an important concept for space sustainability, and has been shown to be necessary to control the unstable growth rate of debris in low Earth orbit (LEO) \cite{inter-agencyspacedebriscoordinationcommitteePeacefulUsesOuter1993,bastidavirgiliActiveDebrisRemoval2013}. Several studies have assessed the technical and economic feasibility of ADR \cite{ledkovReviewContactContactless2022,bonnalActiveDebrisRemoval2013}, and the last decade has seen multiple ADR demonstration missions.


Prior to such missions, autonomous rendezvous and docking capabilities were demonstrated through on-orbit servicing missions such as JAXA's ETS-VII in 1997 (\cite{mokunoInorbitDemonstrationRendezvous2004}), with the knowledge gained proving essential to the development of today's ADR concepts \cite{jaekelDesignOperationalElements2018}. ClearSpace-1, a mission led by ClearSpace under ESA contract, aims to be the first to capture and de-orbit a real piece of space debris: a defunct Vespa upper stage \cite{ClearSpace12025}. Other missions such as Surrey Space Centre's RemoveDEBRIS mission (2018), and Astroscale's ELSA-d mission (2021), successfully deployed and then captured simulated debris objects \cite{forshawRemoveDEBRISInorbitActive2016,ELSAdAstroscaleSecuring2025}. A further mission by Astroscale in 2024 (ADRAS-J), focused on proximity operation testing; it approached to within 15m of a debris object  \cite{ADRASJAstroscaleSecuring2025}. This is the closest approach to-date by a commercial satellite to a debris object. Unlike on-orbit servicing missions, the targets of ADR missions, are by definition uncooperative. This means that additional care must be taken during any close proximity operations, to minimise the risk of collision with the debris \cite{shanReviewComparisonActive2016}. Applicable standards for the mitigation of space debris are NASA-STD-8719.14C in North America (\cite{nationalaeronauticcsandspaceadministrationNASASTD871914C2021}), and ISO 24113 in Europe (after adoption by ECSS) \cite{ecssspacedebrisworkinggroupAdoptionNoticeISO09}. Both of these standards state any debris greater than 10cm poses a serious risk of damage, were it to be involved in a collision. For this reason, companies such as Astroscale have adopted a zero-debris policy during ADR missions, aiming to create no new debris during operations.

One way of minimising the risk of debris creation is to reduce the spin rate differential between a debris object and the chaser spacecraft \cite{bonnalActiveDebrisRemoval2013}. Nishida suggests that a spin rate differential less than $3\degree /s$ is necessary to easily capture a debris object using a robotic arm \cite{nishidaStrategyCapturingTumbling2011}. Indeed, it has become generally accepted that the ability to detumble debris is necessary for any rigid-connection ADR concept such at robotic arm grappling \cite{ledkovReviewContactContactless2022a}. Several methods for detumbling space debris have been proposed: nets and tethers \cite{oconnorDetumblingSpaceDebris2017}; laser ablation \cite{vetrisanoDetumblingLargeSpace2015}; flexible brushes \cite{chengStrategyControlRobotic2019}; eddy currents \cite{ortizgomezEddyCurrentsApplied2015}; and gas thruster plume impingement \cite{ferrariGasPlumeImpingement2014,nakajimaDetumblingSpaceDebris2016,petersCOBRAContactlessDetumbling2016}.
    
It is this final method that is the focus of this paper. Nakajima highlights the favourable properties of plume impingement as a detumbling method, namely that no physical contact between debris and chaser satellite is needed during detumbling \cite{nakajimaDetumblingSpaceDebris2016}. Further, a chaser satellite will generally already possess the capability to perform plume impingement, by use of its reaction control system (RCS) thrusters. Figure \ref{method_fig_plumeImpingementDetumbleConcept} demonstrates how these thrusters may be used to impart a detumbling moment onto a debris object.

Plume impingement as a method of detumbling has been studied in-depth as the COBRA concept, developed as part of ESA's SysNova technology assessment scheme \cite{petersCOBRAContactlessDetumbling2016}. The COBRA concept looked specifically at detumbling ENVISAT, a large Earth-Observation satellite, using a 5N thruster. ENVISAT has been the focus of similar ADR studies by Ferrari and Vetrisano \cite{ferrariGasPlumeImpingement2014,vetrisanoDetumblingLargeSpace2015a}. Its large size, large mass, and common (sun-synchronous) orbit, greatly increases its associated risk. Indeed, it has been identified that almost all major spacecraft targets for ADR lie in the region of sun-synchronous orbits: inclinations of 95-100\textdegree, and altitudes of 500-1000km \cite{liouEngineeringTechnologyChallenges2013}. Servadio et al. determined ten priority targets for ADR in this orbital region, following the creation of a risk index based on the debris' mass and residual lifetime, as well as the background density across its orbit \cite{servadioRiskIndexOptimal2024}. All ten of the targets identified were defunct US satellites launched between 1964-1972. The characteristic lengths of these satellites range from 1.2-4.2m, and the dry masses from 50-192kg. For this reason, this work focuses on satellites of this scale, and it has been assumed that the general shape of a target debris object resembles Figure \ref{method_fig_plumeImpingementDetumbleConcept}- specifically a central 'box' with deployed solar panels.

Most studies on debris detumbling via plume impingement focus on the scenario from a guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) standpoint. That is, they concentrate on the force and time required to generate sufficient torque to detumble a debris object. The aim of this work was instead to determine whether there is a significant risk of debris breakup during a detumbling sequence. This was achieved by analysing the combined thermal and mechanical effects of a thruster plume for two specific debris breakup scenarios: the impingement of an embrittled, micro-cracked solar panel; and impingement effects on an adhesive layer of a solar panel.


Section \ref{Section_Background} presents a background of methods commonly used to analyse plume impingement scenarios, in particular analytical techniques. Section \ref{Section_Method} presents the methodology used in this work to model the plume, its impingement, and the failure mechanics considered when determining the risk of debris breakup. Section \ref{Section_Results} presents the results of this work, and are discussed along with an evaluation of the methodology in Section \ref{Section_Discussion}. The findings are concluded and recommendations for future work suggested in section \ref{Section_Conclusion}.

\section{Background}
\label{Section_Background}



Dettleff presents a comprehensive review of techniques to study plume flows and plume impingement \cite{dettleffPlumeFlowPlume1991}. In it he explores different methodologies and their application in different regions of a plume flow. A key concept of plume flows in space, is the effect of large density variations: from relatively high densities at the exit of the nozzle, to extremely low densities in the far field vacuum. The collision frequency $\nu$ between particles in the plume is proportional to the density, and hence far away from the nozzle exit there becomes insufficient molecular collisions to maintain equilibrium within the flow. With this comes the breakdown of continuum models characterised by the Navier-Stokes equations. That is, there are insufficient particular collisions to transfer kinetic energy, and the adiabatic relation $T \propto P^{((1-\gamma)/ \gamma)}$ no longer holds. Instead, the flow begins to behave as free molecules, described by the Maxwell-Boltzmann equation of kinetic theory \cite{pittPlumeImpingementStudies2022}. The transition of a flow from 'continuum' to 'free molecular' behaviour is commonly described by the Knudsen number, defined as the mean free path normalised by a characteristic length: $Kn = \lambda/L$. The mean free path of a particle is the average distance between travelled by a particle between two collisions, defined mathematically as:

 
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = u/ \nu =  \frac{m}{\sqrt{2} \rho {\sigma}_T} 
    \label{eq_back_meanFreePath}
\end{equation}

It is appropriate to note here that all parameters used throughout this paper, and their subscripts, are defined in the nomenclature section; definitions are only reiterated in the report's body where necessary for clarity. Figure \ref{fig_back_flowRegions} shows the different regions of a flow as defined by Knudsen number, and the typical values of Kn for which each region is valid. 

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\columnwidth]{Figures/FlowRegionsWithDims.pdf}
    \caption{Plume flow regions, and their characteristic Knudsen and Bird parameter ranges.}
    \label{fig_back_flowRegions}
  \end{figure}

The choice of characteristic length used to define $Kn$ differs across plume studies. In plume impingement studies, where the focus is on gas-surface interactions, it is more common to use the length from the nozzle exit to the impinged surface or a characteristic length of the surface itself. This way, the choice of $L$ allows the impingement model to reflect the equations of motion applicable over the length scale of the impinged surface (i.e. continuum and Navier-Stokes, or free-molecular and kinetic theory). $Kn$ then, is particularly suited to defining the nature in which a gas plume will impinge onto a surface. Bird introduced another parameter that more explicitly describes the breakdown of equilibrium within a flow (rarefaction), that is not subject to the discrepancies introduced by needing to choose a normalisation factor. Bird's Parameter is defined as

\begin{equation}
    P = \frac{u}{\nu \rho} \cdot |\frac{d\rho}{dr}|
    \label{back_eq_BirdParam}
\end{equation}

Specifically, it describes the onset of thermal non-equilibrium of the flow, whereby the plume temperature plateaus to a minimum, even as density continues to decay. This 'freezing' of the flow more closely describes its rarefaction, and is directly related to the breakdown of continuum assumptions. As such, many models use $P$ to define the limits of where continuum and free-molecular \textit{plume} models are used, and $Kn$ to define where different \textit{impingement} models are used. 

The Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method is accepted as the best current method to describe the transition and free molecular plume regions. DSMC is a phenomenological method that models individual particular collisions, rather than assuming continuous distribution functions of velocity \cite{birdMolecularGasDynamics2003}. This, unsurprisingly, can result in high computational cost if the number of particular collisions is high \cite{landauGuideMonteCarlo2021}. 

There are also several difficulties associated with experimental techniques of measuring plume impingement, primarily due to the difficulty of maintaining realistic orbital vacuum levels whilst firing a thruster \cite{dettleffBasicsPlumeImpingement2011}. This limits the mass flux that a thruster can eject during testing. This limit is seen in many experimental results reported in literature, which use small RCS thrusters, in particular the ERNO 0.5N thruster \cite{boydModelingSmallHydrazine1990,boydAssessmentImpingementEffects1989,leggePitotPressureHeattransfer1986,leggeModellingControlThruster1982}.Further difficulties arise when studying hydrazine thrusters. The dangers of hydrazine are perhaps most aptly described by Clark who remarked its ability to "eat through everything - metal, rubber, glass, and even the engineer who has to work with it" \cite{clarkIgnitionInformalHistory2017}.

Luckily, in his review, Dettleff states that it is possible to describe a plume flow to a sufficiently exact degree using analytical methods. Such analytical methods are particularly applicable for hypersonic, vacuum situations. Commonly in analytical methods, the flow is modelled to expand radially with straight streamlines from a point source at the centre of the exit plane \cite{mehtaUnderexpandedSupersonicPlume2011}.In these scenarios, the expansion of the plume at the nozzle exit is large in angle and Mach number, such that the flow appears to expand radially with straight streamlines from a point source at the centre of the exit plane \cite{mehtaUnderexpandedSupersonicPlume2011}. From this assumption, the decay of density of the plume is characterised in its most basic formulation as:

\begin{equation}
    \rho(r, \phi ) = C \frac{1}{r^2} f(\phi)
    \label{eq_back_densityDecayBasic}
\end{equation}


It can be seen that the density decays both with radial distance from the nozzle, and also with angle from the centreline. In most cases the plume can be assumed axisymmetric about the nozzle centreline, and hence a 2D plume model is sufficient. Peters and Olmos identified two popular analytical models used for plume impingement modelling: Simons' model and Fehse's model \cite{petersCOBRAContactlessDetumbling2016,simonsEffectNozzleBoundary1972,fehseAutomatedRendezvousDocking2003}. Both take the form of Eq. \ref{eq_back_densityDecayBasic}. Fehse's model describes pressure distribution, and was used by Ferrari et al when analysing the detumbling of ENVISAT \cite{ferrariGasPlumeImpingement2014}. Simon's model has been used more in plume impingement studies, in part due to its ability to model the viscous effects in boundary layer region of the plume. Simons' defines two important angles for the flow: $\theta_{lim}$ the most extreme angle of the flow, where the velocity approaches zero; and $\theta_0$, which is the limit of the isentropic core. Figure \ref{fig_back_flowRegions} shows these two angles. A further extension to Simons' model was presented by Legge and Boettcher, primarily aiming to better capture the effects of rarefaction within the boundary layer of the plume \cite{leggeModellingControlThruster1982}. A freezing surface at $P=2$ was used to define the onset of the free molecular region. It is assumed that no molecular collisions occur downstream of this freezing surface, and hence local flow velocity and temperature hit maximum and minimum values respectively. 

In the same work, Legge and Boettcher use the method of Schaaf and Chambre to describe impingement of the plume onto a surface in the free molecular region. Schaaf and Chambre presented the use of three accommodation coefficients to describe the degree to which the interaction of particles with a surface is specular or diffuse \cite{chambreFlowRarefiedGases2017}: thermal accommodation coefficient $\alpha_e$ to describe energy transfer; and normal and tangential momentum accommodation coefficients $\alpha_n$ and $\alpha_t$. Figure \ref{back_fig_specularVsDiffuse} shows the distinction between specular and diffuse collisions. In specular reflection, there is no momentum or energy transfer from the particle to the surface. In fully diffuse interactions, the incident molecule is bought to equilibrium state with the surface.

    \label{back_fig_specularVsDiffuse}
\end{figure}

Monopropellant hydrazine thrusters have been the focus of multiple plume impingement studies, in particular Boyd's work \cite{boydAssessmentImpingementEffects1989,boydModelingSmallHydrazine1990}. Like Boyd, this analysis used Simons' method to model the plume flow, as it was presented and extended by Legge and Boettcher \cite{leggeModellingControlThruster1982}. NASA's \textit{Chemical Equilibrium with Applications} (CEA) code, was used to determine the input parameters needed for Simons' model \cite{josephbanksChemicalEquilibriumApplications2023}. The inputs to CEA itself are those of Table \ref{method_table_ceaInputs}. The outputs of Simons' model were used to determine the energy and momentum transfer onto the impinged debris surface. Schaaf and Chambre's method for determining energy and momentum transfer in a free molecular flow has been used to model the plume impingement \cite{chambreFlowRarefiedGases2017}. The calculated impingement effects were then used to determine peak stresses and maximum temperature increases during an example detumbling sequence. From these results, the risk of debris breakup for two specific scenarios could be assessed; fracture propagation on a micro-cracked solar panel glass layer; and the melting of an adhesive layer of a solar panel. The general workflow of this methodology is shown in Fig. \ref{method_fig_workflow}.

\subsection{Determining Nozzle Exit Plane Properties}

Simons' model requires, as inputs, flow properties at the exit plane of the nozzle; Legge notes the particular importance of knowing $\gamma$ accurately at this location \cite{leggeModellingControlThruster1982}. To determine the relevant parameters, the \textit{rocket} solver of \textit{CEArun rev4} was used \cite{josephbanksChemicalEquilibriumApplications2023}. This program computes throat and exit plane parameters, and takes as input: chamber pressure; reactant species; reactant temperature; and nozzle expansion ratio. Notably, CEA does not allow chamber temperature to be specified, and instead a reactant temperature of $579.35K$ was specified to achieve the desired chamber temperature $T_0=1200K$. CEA defines the chamber conditions as those where velocity is zero. Hence, 'chamber' and 'stagnation' conditions are identical here. The run file of the CEA analysis can be found in the Appendix. The reactant species chosen for the CEA analysis was liquid Hydrazine "$N_2H_4(L)$" (as opposed to the gaseous form), as it is the state most commonly found in monopropellant RCS subsystems used on spacecraft to date \cite{greatrixLiquidPropellantRocketEngines2012,suttonRocketPropulsionElements2011}.

%Should talk a little bit about how CEA works
The CEA \textit{rocket} solver first models the chemical reaction of the chamber, and subsequently models the isentropic expansion of the reacted gas mixture through a nozzle. The chemical reaction is computed by minimising the Gibbs energy, and the isentropic expansion is calculated according to isentropic flow relations, found in common aerodynamics references such as Anderson \cite{andersonFundamentalsAerodynamics2011}. The \textit{rocket} solver can be run in two modes: frozen flow, where the chemical composition is fixed after combustion; or a shifting equilibrium flow, where the flow properties are re-solved for equilibrium throughout the nozzle length. In this analysis, a shifting-equilibrium model was used, in order to capture some of the condensation and recombination effects known to occur in the nozzle of small thrusters \cite{sahamiEffectsNonequilibriumCondensation2022}. The better estimation of product species mass fractions also aided in subsequent analysis of Knudsen number. Both the chemical reaction, and isentropic expansion solver introduce assumptions about the nozzle flow:

\begin{itemize}
    \item complete combustion occurs in the chamber
    \item thermodynamic equilibrium is achieved at all points in the combustion chamber and nozzle
    \item the flow is a steady with time, and an ideal gas
    \item the flow expands isentropically with no viscous boundary layer
    \item no shockwaves form in the nozzle
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Plume Model}

As stated, Simons' method was used for modelling the thruster plume. This model was chosen firstly because its analytical nature leads to small computational cost, compared to numerical methods such as MOC and DSMC. Secondly, Peters and Olmos showed that Simon's model aligned more closely with DSMC solutions, compared with Fehse's analytical model \cite{petersCOBRAContactlessDetumbling2016}. Simons' model describes the decay of density in the plume:


\begin{equation}
    \rho (r, \phi) = \rho (r, \phi=0) \cdot f(\phi) = \rho^* A_P \left( \frac{r*}{r} \right) ^{2} \cdot f(\phi)
    \label{method_eq_densitySimons}
\end{equation}

The angular decay function is defined differently for the isentropic plume core ($\phi \leq \phi_0$), compared to the viscous boundary layer ($\phi_0 < \phi \leq \phi_{\lim}$):

\begin{equation} 
f(\phi) =
\begin{cases} 
\cos^{\frac{2}{\gamma -1}} \left( \frac{\pi \phi_0}{2 \phi_{\lim}} \right), & \phi \leq \phi_0 \\
f(\phi=\phi_0) e^{-c_{\rho} (\phi - \phi_0)}, & \phi_0 < \phi \leq \phi_{\lim} \\
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

%Hence, $\phi_0$ defines the edge of the core, and $\phi_{\lim}$ the edge of the boundary layer. The two angles are defined as:
%
%\begin{equation}
%    \phi_0 = \phi_{lim} \left( 1 - \frac{2}{\pi} \left( \frac{2 \delta_E}{r_E} \right)^{\frac{\gamma -1}{\gamma +1}} \right)
%    \label{method_eq_phi0}
%\end{equation}
%
%\begin{equation}
%    \phi_{lim} = \nu(M=\infty) - \nu(M_E)
%    \label{method_eq_phiLim}
%\end{equation}

$\phi_{lim}$ and $\phi_0$ are calculated using the Prandtl-Mayer angle and boundary layer thickness $\delta_E$, evaluated at the nozzle exit. Since CEA assumes inviscid flow, $\delta_E$ was estimated from Ref. \cite{leggeModellingControlThruster1982}. $c_{\rho}$ and $A_P$ are plume constants affecting angular and radial decay respectively, and are defined in Eqs. \ref{method_eq_cRho} and \ref{method_eq_Ap}. ${\bar{u}}_{lim}$ was approximated as ${\bar{u}}_{lim} = 0.75 u_{lim}$.

\begin{equation}
c_{\rho} = A_P \left( \frac{\gamma +1}{\gamma - 1} \right)^{1/2}  \frac{2 {\bar{u}}_{lim}}{u_{lim}} \left( \frac{r_E}{2 \delta_E} \right) ^{\frac{\gamma -1}{\gamma +1}}
\label{method_eq_cRho}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    A_P = \frac{u*}{2 u_{lim} \int_{0}^{\phi_{lim}} f(\phi) sin{\phi}  \,d\phi }
    \label{method_eq_Ap}
\end{equation}


These equations allow the density field of the plume core to be defined, from which other flow properties such as temperature, pressure, Mach number, and velocity, could be found from isentropic relations. Fig. \ref{method_fig_workflow} shows the necessary outputs from the plume model are $\rho$, $T$, $\gamma$, and $u$. $\gamma$ is assumed constant within the entire plume, and $\rho$ is shown above to decay throughout the plume. The change of $T$ and $u$ is more complex. As discussed in Section \ref{Section_Background}, the breakdown of thermal equilibrium of the flow leads to a freezing (minimum) temperature and limiting (maximum) velocity. $P=2$ was used to define the freezing surface in this work, at which the plume was assumed free molecular, and $u = u_{lim}$ and $T=T_f$ fixed. $u_{lim}$ is constant within the plume core, whereas $T_f$ decays with $\phi$. Legge's proposal for effective stagnation conditions was used for the boundary layer: $\rho_0$, $p_0$, and $T_0$ were assumed to decay exponentially with $\phi$ (when $\phi > \phi_0$), to account for viscous effects. Consequently, both $u_{lim}$ and $T_f$ are functions $\phi$ in the boundary layer. 

    
\subsection{Plume Impingement Model}

The gas-surface interaction between the plume and a debris surface causes a transfer of momentum and energy. The degree to which this transfer takes place for a particular collision depends on the accommodation coefficients introduced in Section \ref{Section_Background}: momentum coefficients $\alpha_n$ and $\alpha_t$, and thermal (energy) coefficient $\alpha_e$. Schaff highlighted the implicit assumption that $\alpha_e$ describes energy transfer from all molecular degrees of freedom (translational, rotational, vibrational) evenly \cite{chambreFlowRarefiedGases2017}, and that this was found to be approximately true for translational and rotational components. However, the vibrational energy was altered to a lesser extent during observed impingement \cite{euckenHandUndJahrbuch1950}. The temperature required to excite the fundamental frequencies $\omega_e$ of $NH_3$ and $N_2$, defined as $ \omega_e = 3336cm^{-1}$ and $ 2359cm^{-1}$ respectively \cite{irikuraExperimentalVibrationalZeroPoint2007,koopsMeasurementInterpretationAbsolute1983}, was then estimated by $T_{vib} = \hbar \omega_e / k_B$ to be 4792K and 3392K for the two species. From Table \ref{method_table_ceaInputs}, the maximum gas temperature considered in this analysis was 1200K. Hence, the energy contained within vibrational modes of $NH_3$ and $N_2$ was small within the considered plume flow, minimising errors in $\alpha_e$ relating to vibrational energy transfer.

Considering the rotational energy modes: rotational constants of  $9.4443 cm^{-1}$ and $1.9982 cm^{-1}$ for $NH_3$ and $N_2$ respectively were used, yielding activation temperatures $T_{rot} = B/ k_B$ of 13.58K and 2.87K for the two gas species \cite{huberConstantsDiatomicMolecules1979}. Hence, rotational modes could not be considered frozen. As a result, the assumption of hyperthermal flow used in Ref. \cite{petersCOBRAContactlessDetumbling2016} has been dropped in this analysis, in favour of Schaaf and Chambre's momentum transfer model:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    p = \frac{dF_n}{dA} = \frac{1}{2} \rho u^2 \frac{1}{S^2} \Bigg[ &
    \left( \frac{2 - \alpha_n}{\sqrt{\pi}}  S \sin{\theta} + \frac{\alpha_n}{2} \sqrt{\frac{T_w}{T}} \right) 
    e^{- (S \sin{\theta})^2} \\
    &\quad + \left(  (2 - \alpha_n)\left( (S \sin{\theta})^2 + \frac{1}{2} \right) + 
    \frac{\alpha_n}{2} \sqrt{\pi \frac{T_w}{T}} (S\sin{\theta}) \right)
    \cdot \Big( 1 + \operatorname{erf} (S\sin{\theta}) \Big)   
    \Bigg]
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq_method_Fn_differential}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    %\begin{aligned}
        \tau = \frac{dF_t}{dA} = \frac{\alpha_t \rho u^2 \cos{\theta}}{2 \sqrt{\pi}} \Bigg( e^{-(S\sin{\theta})^2}  + \sqrt{\pi} \Big(S \sin{\theta}\Big) \Big(1 + \operatorname{erf} (S \sin{\theta})\Big)    \Bigg)
    %\end{aligned}
    \label{eq_method_Ft_differential}
\end{equation}

Where $\frac{dF}{dA}$ has been split into its normal and tangential components, which represent meaningful values of pressure $p$ and shear stress $\tau$, that vary with the incidence angle of the surface $\theta$ relative to the plume centreline. Eqs. \ref{eq_method_Ft_differential} and \ref{eq_method_Fn_differential} are valid only in the free molecular regime, where the velocity of the plume is considered to follow a maxwellian distribution. The same assumption is used in the derivation of Schaaf and Chambre's energy transfer model:

\begin{equation}
    \dot{q} =  \alpha_e \rho \mathcal{R} T \sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{R} T}{2 \pi}} \bigg( \bigg[ S^2 + \frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1} - \frac{\gamma + 1 }{2( \gamma -1)} \frac{T_w}{T} \bigg] 
     \cdot \bigg[ e^{-(S \sin{\theta})^2} + \sqrt{\pi} ( S \sin{\theta} ) \{1 + erf ( S \sin{\theta}) \}  \bigg]
     - \frac{1}{2} e^{-(S \sin{\theta})^2} \bigg) 
    \label{method_eq_heatFluxIntegral}
\end{equation}

Where speed ratio $S$ represents the ratio of the local flow velocity $u$, to the most probable gas speed as given by a maxwellian distribution, and is defined in Eq. \ref{eq_method_speedRatio}. All accommodation coefficients were taken as 0.95.

\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{u}{\sqrt{2 \mathcal{R} T}}
    \label{eq_method_speedRatio}
\end{equation}

A further simplification to the radiation modelling was made by assuming that the surface was in equilibrium just before impingement. Normally in LEO, the solar heat flux, and heat flux from Earth infrared emissions and albedo, all change during an orbital period \cite{gilmoreSpacecraftThermalControl2002}. Instead, it was assumed that these heat fluxes were constant during the impingement sequence such that the surface reached thermal balance at a temperature of $T_w = 300K$. Hence, nodes were modelled to radiate to a representative isothermal space node at $T_{space} = 300K$, to satisfy the thermal balance requirement. 

An explicit Euler finite forward difference time-stepping scheme was used to compute the temperature change per time step $\Delta t$, and is shown for a surface node $m$ and adjacent nodes $n$ in Eq. \ref{method_eq_FiniteDiff}. The thermal capacitance of each node was calculated as $C_m = \rho_m dA_m z_m c_{\rho}$. Similarly, the Conductance $K_{mn}$ between nodes $m$ and $n$ is defined as $K_{mn} = k_m \frac{dA_m}{dn_{mn}}$.

\begin{equation}
    T_m^{j+1} = T_m^j + \frac{\Delta t}{C_m} \left( \sum_n K_{mn} (T_n^j - T_m^j) + Q_m^{\theta} - \sigma_B \epsilon_m dA_m \left( (T_m^j)^4 - T_{\text{space}}^4 \right) \right)
    \label{method_eq_FiniteDiff}
\end{equation}


 Eq. \ref{method_eq_FiniteDiff} shows the temperature change to be dependent on both material and geometric properties. A surface $1m$ in width $W$ and $2m$ in length $L$ was considered in this analysis, discretised into mesh elements $0.2m$ in height and width. Hence, $dn=0.2m$, and $dA = 0.04m$. An emissivity $\epsilon = 0.9$ was assumed, and a range of thicknesses $z$ were considered. Generalised material properties of three types of glass used in space solar panels are shown in Table \ref{method_tab_GlassMatProps}; properties have been collated from Refs. \cite{gilmoreSpacecraftThermalControl2002} and \cite{ansysAnsysGrantaEduPack2024}. $\dot{q}_m^{\theta}$ was calculated at each time step for each node. $\theta$ at time step $j$ was evaluated based on the debris spin rate $\omega$, such that $\theta^j = \theta^{j-1} + \Delta t \omega^j$. The spin rate was assumed to decrease linearly from $5\degree /s$ at the start of the detumbling sequence, to $0 \degree /s$ after 1800 seconds, based on the results in Ref. \cite{petersCOBRAContactlessDetumbling2016}.

\begin{equation}
    G_c = \frac{K_c^2}{E}
\end{equation}

\section{Discussion}
\label{Section_Discussion}

The results presented in Section \ref{Section_Results} show the effects of energy transfer to be much greater than momentum transfer. However, neither leading to significant risk of debris breakup for the two evaluated scenarios during the example detumbling scenario. The results are discussed below, in particular the validity and limitations of Simons' model in the free molecular regime, and the effect on the presented results. 

Considering first the impingement results, Fig. \ref{fig_res_PressureAndShearWithAoA} shows the maximum impinged stress due to momentum to be approximately $2Pa$. Peak shear stresses are approximately ten times the pressure forces, a result confirmed by other reported results using Schaaf and Chambre's method to calculate drag and lift for flat plate \cite{storchAerodynamicDisturbancesSpacecraft2002}. Nevertheless, both $p$ and $\tau$ are still small. $2Pa$ is approximately $10^5$ times greater than stresses resultant from solar radiation pressure and atmospheric drag at 500km orbit \cite{gilmoreSpacecraftThermalControl2002}, but it is still well below typical glass yield strength exceeding $100MPa$ \cite{ansysAnsysGrantaEduPack2024}. From Fig. \ref{fig_res_CritFracStress}, it is clear that stresses induced due to thermal expansion of the surface are much more significant: in the order of $0.3-1.1MPa$. It is to be expected that the effects of energy transfer are of a larger scale, compared to those of momentum transfer. As discussed in Section \ref{Section_Method}, there is a transfer of translational and rotational energy of an impinging molecule. Considering only the translational energy, this kinetic energy is $\propto u^2$. On the other hand, momentum transfer is only proportional to $u$. In the free molecular region when $u_{lim}=2382m/s$ is reached, this leads to energy transfer at least $10^3$ times greater than momentum transfer. 
Fig. \ref{fig_res_CritFracStress} shows that for the case of fracture propagation, the total impinged stress becomes close to the critical fracture stress for large initial crack lengths (particularly for borosilicate glass). However, at more common crack lengths of the $\mu m$ scale, the safety margin for this case is still in the order of 10-100. 
A maximum temperature increase for a 1mm thick surface of $13K$ was reached for the continuous impingement presented in Fig. \ref{fig_res_TempIncreaseWithThickness}, and $12K$ for the detumbling sequence of Fig. \ref{fig_res_TemScenario}. These temperature increases are less than typical transient temperature ranges experienced by external surfaces during an orbital period. Further, for the scenario considered, the heat flux was an order of magnitude less than that typical from solar radiation in LEO. It is clear from this that there is no risk of causing adhesive melting, if that adhesive is rated for the spacecrafts operations. There is also a clear mitigation to further reduce the risk of debris breakup: where plume impingement be only performed during eclipse periods, such that the total heat flux imparted onto the surface is never greater than during an illuminated, non-impinged case.

Looking further at the plume model, analytical plume models of the form of Eq. \ref{eq_back_densityDecayBasic} are known to cause underpredictions of plume temperature, due to the effects of flow condensation not captured in the flowfield \cite{dettleffBasicsPlumeImpingement2011}. Some mitigation of condensation effects was achieved in this work by using CEA with the equilibrium solver, which accounts for flow condensation in the nozzle - increasing the predicted exit temperature. Nevertheless, the plume model itself assumes no condensation. Results for a 0.5N thruster reported in Ref. \cite{boydModelingSmallHydrazine1990} showed an underprediction of impinged pressure of $~ 10^2 Pa$. Whilst more significant than the errors relating to the transition regime discussed above, even if $p$ were to be underpredicted by $10^2 Pa$ this would still only cause an underprediction of $\sigma_peak$ of 0.1\% for the worst case of borosilicate glass. Consequently, the peak stresses experienced by the surface would still be below the critical stress for fracture propagation.

Another possible source of error in the plume model  was the use of average gas properties of the reaction products, rather than modelling $N_2$ and $NH_3$ separately. Whilst the average property approach is generally followed in plume impingement studies using analytical models, it comes with the implicit assumption that the mass fractions of reactant products is constant throughout the plume. Experimental evidence gathered by Allen et al. found that in fact, product species of lower molecular mass tended to be pushed to greater angles of $\phi$ and into the boundary layer, and a concentration of heavier species was found close to the plume centreline \cite{allenStudySpeciesSeparation1986}. This would have the effect of increasing density and subsequently $p$, $\tau$, and $\dot{q}$ close to the plume centreline. The precise impact of this species concentration is not possible to determine without more involved numerical methods like DSMC.


\section{Future Work}

It is clear that a lack of numerical or experimental results for direct comparison, limits the analytical model's results presented in this work. Future work should focus on the generation of comparable results using numerical or experimental methods. A hybrid CFD/DSMC approach seems the most suitable, like that of Nakajima \cite{nakajimaDetumblingSpaceDebris2016}. In addition, DSMC simulations would allow the study of more complex geometries, where multiple particle reflections become important. In particular, greater refinement to the layer stack of a solar panel, may allow for better estimations of heat transfer across different layers (and materials) of a solar panel.

From results found in this work, it is clear that energy transfer and hence momentum flux are the major cause of any impingement effects. Hence, it makes sense for additional work to be done on improving the thermal solver used in this work. In particular, coupling of the plume model outputs of this work with more powerful thermal software suites like Airbus SYSTEMA and ESATAN could allow not only for the modelling of complex geometries, but also for the assumption of non-impingement thermal balance to be dropped. Instead, impingement could be modelled as part of a realistic orbital cycle of incident heat fluxes.

Of course, further work may be performed by considering more impingement scenarios. In particular, the consideration of impingement effects onto pressurised fuel tanks is a possible situation that warrants investigation. Finally, the merging of the method and models presented in this work, with models presented in work such as Peters and Olmos \cite{petersCOBRAContactlessDetumbling2016}, would improve the modelling of the rotation rate of the impinged surface, as well as allow for less simplistic tumbling patterns to be simulated. 



\section{Conclusions}

The results of a plume impingement study, particularly the thermal and mechanical effect of an RCS thruster on a debris object, have been presented. Critically, they suggest the risk of debris breakup from plume impingement is low. For a distance of 10m between the thruster nozzle and impinged surface, peak stresses due to momentum transfer from the plume to the surface were found to be approximately $3.64Pa$. Energy transfer for the same condition caused peak heat fluxes of $146.5W/m^2$ for a surface temperature of $300K$. his value is 1-2 times less than typical solar fluxes in LEO, and hence it has been suggested that one possible way to mitigate against debris breakup would be to perform plume impingement only in eclipse periods, ensuring the condition during impingement is never worse (thermally) than that of an illuminated orbital period. Peak momentum transfer was found to occur for an incidence angle of $46\degree$ between the surface and plume centreline, whilst maximum heat flux occurred at an incidence angle of $90 \degree$. Two possible causes of debris breakup were considered. For an example detumbling scenario where a 1mm thick glass surface was impinged, the maximum temperature increase was $12K$, and the peak induced stress in the surface in the order of 1MPa. Neither of these effects were found to cause debris breakup for the three glass materials considered.

However, the lack of numerical or experimental results able to directly verify the analytical modelling presented in this paper must limit confidence in the reported results. Underpredictions of heat flux and pressure are known to occur for the analytical Simons' plume model used in this work. Further, simplifications to the modelling of the plume gas mixture, and the assumption that the impinged surface will always be in the free molecular regime, both increase errors in the results. It has been shown that for many cases, these errors are insignificant compared to the safety margins found for the example scenarios. However, more complex modelling yb use of numerical techniques such as DSMC would give greater estimation of the magnitude of these errors, and give greater confidence to the results in general.
